Design the process · four weeks, six groups, sixteen objections

Listen first, show each household what changes, change the project in public, then commit in writing.

The four weeks are for changing the project in response to what residents say, and for showing that it changed. The legal permitting process takes a year or more after that; these four weeks shape the application that goes into it.

Gate. At least three objections have changed the project, each change certified by the three mayors (from week 3, by the monitoring committee).

03

Change the project in public

The change log goes public: every change made to the project, who asked for it and what it costs. A revised site plan if the workshops changed anything. The neighbors' charter opens for signature, the 48-hour contact is named, the monitoring committee is set up with residents on it, residents nominate candidates for the fund's board, and co-op pre-registration opens.

Gate. The charter is open for signature and the committee is set up.

04

Commit in writing

The eight commitments as a signed document, each with how it will be measured, including the €5M seed and the fund's floor as dated obligations; the draft co-op statutes published with them. A final meeting in each commune: the project as it now stands next to the day 1 version. The register, the change log and the commitments go to the national public-debate commission (CNDP), whose independent guarantor, requested in week 1, oversees the consultation that follows once appointed. The legal procedure itself opens when the application is filed with the Préfecture.

Gate. The day-28 test passes: every objection answered with a source, five certified changes in the change log, no number without a source, a meeting offered to every charter-zone household.

The day-28 test

It can fail. If it fails, the application is not filed until it passes.

By the end of week four

  1. Every objection in the register has a published answer with a source.
  2. The change log has at least five entries: changes made to the project because of what residents said, each with who asked for it, each certified by the three mayors or, from week 3, the monitoring committee.
  3. No published number is without a source.
  4. Every household in the charter zone has been offered a meeting.

The team does the work behind all four, and the mayors or the monitoring committee certify the changes, so the test can fail. What the team can only influence, such as how many households it actually met, how many sign the charter, and whether ten uninvolved residents can find in two minutes what changes at their address and name one change made because of what people said, is listed in the metrics below as targets, not as the test.

Week by week

What happens, who is in the room, and what has to be true before the next week starts.

01Listen before you draw

  • Day 1. Publish the case for the project, the eight commitments as numbers, the site plan, and the draft terms of the local benefit fund and the heat co-op. No pictures yet. Measure the night noise background at the nearest façades.
  • Door to door in the charter zone: every household on the nearest streets, within about 1 km of the lots. One evening meeting per commune.
  • Open the objection register online, sixteen objections to start; residents can add their own. Ask the CNDP for an independent guarantor; the appointment takes longer than four weeks.
  • Private meetings: the three mayors, Rennes Métropole, the union delegates, the school head, France Nature Environnement (FNE).
  • Gate. The register is public and every objection has a named person answering it.

02Show what changes at your address

  • Launch the address tool: distance, visibility, night noise, construction route, heat phase and saving.
  • Publish the before-and-after pictures of the view from rue du Manoir and from le Bois Noir.
  • Publish the energy plan with the CO₂ figure and the dated steps for switching fuel; invite FNE and the local opposition group to challenge it.
  • Workshops: the neighbors' charter with draft text on the table, the heat tariff, the fund's spending split, the founding of the co-op, the park (what it holds and its name), the construction route. The energy-lab programme is shown to the school head.
  • Gate. At least three objections changed the project, recorded in the change log and each certified by the three mayors (from week 3, by the monitoring committee).

03Change the project in public

  • Publish the change log: every change made, who asked for it, what it costs.
  • Revised site plan if the workshops changed anything: where the power station sits, how high the noise barriers are, the planted lot.
  • Open the neighbors' charter for signature; name the 48-hour contact. Residents nominate candidates for the fund's board; co-op pre-registration opens.
  • Set up the monitoring committee: who sits on it, the residents' seat, when it meets, what it can stop.
  • Gate. The charter is open for signature and the committee is set up.

04Commit in writing

  • Publish the eight commitments as a signed document, each with how it will be measured; the €5M seed and the fund's floor are dated obligations in it; the draft co-op statutes are published alongside.
  • Final public meeting in each commune: the project as it now stands, side by side with day 1.
  • Hand the register, the change log and the commitments to the CNDP for the independent guarantor, who oversees the consultation that follows once appointed; the legal procedure opens when the application is filed with the Préfecture.
  • Gate. The day-28 test passes: every objection answered with a source, five certified changes in the change log, no number without a source, a meeting offered to every charter-zone household.

Risks

What could go wrong, and what happens then.

  • The ZAC's developer does not sell AC 0705, or the factory's owner does not release the park lot. Everything built stands on AC 0705, a plot in the public ZAC La Janais, so Rennes Métropole and its developer are a dependency from day 1; the park depends on the factory's owner. There is no fallback that keeps every commitment. If either says no, the land commitment is reopened in public, in the register, that week.
  • The grid cannot take the surplus at the local substation. Less is exported. The zero-import commitment is unaffected.
  • The CO₂ figure will dominate the debate. State it on day 1, publish the dates by which the fuel switches to biomethane, and do not promise hydrogen.
  • It is unclear whether the public-debate commission has to be involved. Ask it for an independent guarantor in week one anyway.
  • People will say four weeks is theatre. The change log is the answer: it lists what changed because of residents. If nothing changed, say so and why.
  • At €1.1B to €1.3B of capital cost the mandatory referral may apply. If it does, the commission will order a full public debate; the four weeks are then the groundwork for it, not a substitute.
  • People will say the local benefit fund is a bribe. Answer it plainly: it is paid whether or not anyone supports the project, residents and the communes, six of the board's eight seats, decide what it funds, the campus has no veto, and the accounts are published.
  • The local benefit fund is captured by one interest. A resident and commune majority, published accounts, no campus veto, an independent chair.
  • The campus is sold. The local benefit fund's obligation is a covenant that passes to the buyer.
  • Grants go unspent. The reserve is capped at one year's floor; unspent money rolls into retrofit grants.
