Design what the project becomes · what the community owns

At least €1M a year into a fund residents and their communes run, a heat co-op they own, and the lot facing the houses back as a park.

The campus is not something done to the three communes. Part of it is theirs: a local benefit fund paid every year for the life of the campus and run by a board where residents and the communes hold six of eight seats; a share in the company that runs the heat network, with real votes on the price; a 3.7 ha public park on the lot nearest the houses; and a school energy lab and a free community room at Bâtiment 78. Every figure below is derived or an assumption to confirm, and says so.

The local benefit fund

€1 per MWh the station generates, floor €1.0M a year, indexed to French consumer prices, for the life of the campus. €5M at financial close. A board of eight with a resident and commune majority. Audited accounts published every year.

Depends on: legal drafting (fund and covenant).

25to 49% of the network company

The heat co-op

A co-operative (SCIC) that residents, the communes and local firms join at €50 a share, holding a stake in the company that builds and runs the heat-network extension, with a vote on each year's tariff. The campus carries the price risk.

Depends on: the Rennes Métropole concession; legal drafting.

3.7ha of public park

The park on the lot facing the houses

Lot AC 0541, empty today, planted: paths, a play area, an open lawn, and a small winter garden heated by the campus. Open daily, dawn to dusk. Built and kept by the campus for its life; opening date written into the permit, before the first building is energised.

Depends on: the factory owner (release and public access); Rennes Métropole.

The local benefit fund

About 0.1% of the campus's capital cost a year: small enough to be credible, large enough to matter locally. Across about 31,000 residents it is €34 to €56 a person a year, so its job is to target, not to hand out cash.

Who owns the heat network

Residents and the communes own part of the company that runs the heat-network extension, with real votes on the tariff. The campus carries the price risk, so members cannot lose more than their shares.

The co-op. A SCIC (société coopérative d'intérêt collectif), the French co-operative form that lets residents, communes, local firms and the local benefit fund be members together. Legal form to confirm with a lawyer.

Its stake. 25% to 49% of the network company that builds and runs the extension. The rest is held by the operator the Rennes Métropole concession selects. Subject to the Rennes Métropole concession.

Membership. Any connected household can join at €50 a share. The campus company pays the first €50 share for every charter-zone household, so ownership does not depend on ability to pay; the benefit fund keeps to general-interest work (to confirm with a lawyer). The three communes and local firms can join. One member, one vote.

Rights. Board seats in proportion to the stake; a vote on each annual tariff; a share of profits within the SCIC limits. By law a SCIC puts at least 57.5% of each year's surplus into reserves that can never be distributed, so most of the profit stays in the network.

Who carries the risk. The campus sells heat to the network company at a cost-based transfer price fixed in a supply contract. The tariff guarantee (the lower of €54/MWh or half the gas price in the charter zone, at least 10% below gas elsewhere) is underwritten by the campus through that transfer price, not by the co-op. Members can lose at most their shares.

The result. At phase 2 the co-op's share of the network margin is about €3M to €5M a year before the reserve rule (derived; see the fold below); at least 57.5% of it stays in reserve by law, the return on shares is capped, and the main return to members is the lower tariff and the vote on it.

The park on AC 0541

The lot facing the nearest houses becomes a park people walk in. Residents choose its name in week 2; the working name is Parc des Forges.

What it is. 3.7 ha of the 4.4 ha lot, planted; the rail strip at its southern tip stays with the railway. Paths, a play area and an open lawn. A small glass winter garden heated by campus heat, open all year. It is still the planted screen between the houses to the north and the buildings; it just becomes public.

Hours and upkeep. Open daily, dawn to dusk. Built and maintained by the campus for its life; maintenance does not come from the fund. The opening date is written into the permit: before the first building is energised.

Security. A fence along the park's southern edge separates the park from the campus.

Getting in. The lot sits between the railway to the west and the factory to the east; the nearest houses are across the railway and the D177. On the IGN road network the lot is ringed by the industrial zone's public service roads, and a track runs from its north-west corner towards the D177. Public access is through that service road and track, subject to the factory owner and Rennes Métropole. No footbridge or new crossing is promised.

Noise. No separate promise for the park; the noise commitment covers homes.

Bâtiment 78: the energy lab and the community room

Where people learn how the campus works, and where residents meet it. Subject to Rennes Métropole, whose incubator it is.

The energy lab. A room beside the technician academy with a wall of live dashboards from the public meters: grid import and export, heat delivered, water intake, noise at le Bois Noir, CO₂ and the biomethane share.

The school programme. Lesson kits and visits for École Eugène Pottier first, then the other primary schools and collèges in the three communes. One staffed educator, paid by the campus.

The community room. Free to book for local associations. It is also where the fund board meets, the monitoring committee sits, and the 48-hour contact holds office hours. It opens before construction starts, so residents have something visible in the first four weeks, not in year three.

Opening. The room before construction; the lab with the academy, before the campus runs.

How we calculated this

The fund's viability tests and the co-op's economics. Every line is derived or an assumption, and says which.

The fund: can the campus afford it, and is it still paid in a bad year?
Affordability€1.05M to €1.75M a year against a capital cost of €1.1B to €1.3B is about 0.1% a year; the €5M seed is about 0.4% of capex, once. The campus's other annual outgoings on this site: local taxes of €8M to €10M and a carbon cost of about €43M (530,000 t at an allowance price of about €82 per tonne, the press-reported level on 5 October 2026; the price is an assumption to confirm). (derived)
BenchmarkEngland's community-benefit guidance for onshore wind expects "no less than £5,000 per MW of installed capacity per year" (Community Benefits Guidance for Onshore Wind in England, July 2025). For the station's 180 to 200 MW of installed capacity that is about £0.9M to £1.0M, roughly €1.05M to €1.2M; this fund's €1.0M floor is about level with it, and at full output it pays up to €1.75M. No French benchmark is cited because none was opened.
Bad caseThe floor is a fixed €1.0M that ranks ahead of payments to the owners, so it is paid at zero export (the campus still generates 1.05 TWh, €1.05M at €1 per MWh), at a high gas price and at a high carbon price. It would stop only if the campus stopped. (derived)
DurabilityIndexation keeps its value; the reserve rule means one bad year never stops grants. Over 15 years: €5M seed plus about €15M to €26M of annual payments, nominal, before indexation. (derived)
Honest scale€1.05M to €1.75M across about 31,000 residents is €34 to €56 per resident per year.
